# Post C2 — Risk-Based Conditional Access

## What I did
- Created a third Conditional Access policy: **LAB-CA-Risk-Based-MFA**
- Condition: User risk (configured to evaluate sign-ins for user risk level)
- Users: All users, including my own admin account (temporarily, for Report-only testing — same approach used for the first two policies)
- Enable policy: Report-only

## Licensing note
- Before this policy could even be configured, Identity Protection's risk-based conditions were completely unavailable — the license usage page showed 0 entitlement for Entra ID P2 despite an active subscription existing.
- Root cause: the tenant's single purchased P2 seat was over-assigned to two users. Unassigning the extra license restored full Identity Protection functionality, confirmed by the New policy > User risk condition panel loading with real configuration options instead of a licensing block.
- Worth including in the post — diagnosing a licensing/entitlement issue is a realistic day-one task for an IAM analyst, and it's a different skill than the CA config itself.

## What I validated
- Confirmed the policy is correctly scoped (All users, User risk condition configured, Report-only) by reviewing the policy directly in the Conditional Access portal.
- Checked ID Protection > Risky sign-ins for the last 7 days, with Risk state, Risk level (real-time), and Risk level (aggregate) all set to their broadest possible values (High, Medium, Low / all states).
- Result: **No results.** Identity Protection has not flagged a single sign-in as risky, at any risk level, in this tenant.
- Also reviewed the Detection type(s) filter list to confirm the full range of signals Identity Protection is capable of evaluating (anonymous IP address, atypical travel, anomalous token, password spray, leaked credentials, unfamiliar sign-in properties, and others) — none have been triggered.

## What I attempted but hit a real limitation
- Unlike the legacy-auth policy, where I could simulate the exact triggering condition myself (a ROPC-based sign-in classified as a legacy client), Microsoft's risk signals aren't something a single admin can trivially reproduce. They're generated by Microsoft's own threat intelligence and behavioral analysis (leaked credential databases, IP reputation, travel-pattern anomalies) — not a client-type flag I can spoof with a request.
- Because this tenant has exactly one real user and no organic traffic, there is no risk signal available to validate the policy against. This is a limitation of the lab environment, not a misconfiguration.

## Why this matters
- Config-correct is not the same as behavior-verified. I've confirmed the policy is set up exactly as intended, but "no matches" in a Report-only test can mean two very different things: (1) the policy is scoped wrong, or (2) there's genuinely nothing to catch. Distinguishing between those two is exactly the kind of judgment call this exercise is meant to build.
- In this case, I ruled out (1) by directly reviewing the policy configuration and the full Risky sign-ins detection surface at its broadest filter settings, which leaves (2) as the only explanation.

## Decision
- Reviewed the policy configuration and the full Risky sign-ins report at its broadest filter settings (all risk states, all risk levels) with no findings of concern and no unintended matches.
- Switched Enable policy to On. This is a different basis for confidence than the previous two policies: with MFA-Admins and Block-Legacy-Auth, I had at least one real triggering event to validate against. Here, there was no risk signal in this tenant to observe, so enforcement is based on verified configuration correctness rather than observed behavior. Real behavioral validation of this policy will only be possible once this environment has organic multi-user traffic — noting that gap explicitly rather than implying the policy has been proven to fire correctly.
- Re-excluded my break-glass admin account from the policy before enforcing, consistent with the other two policies — enforcement should never be able to lock out the one account needed to fix a misconfigured policy.
